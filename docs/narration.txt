Every month, a small business has to close its books — and the truth is scattered across dozens of documents that never quite agree. Sales and purchase invoices, orders and receipts, bank statements, payments, payroll. Archon pulls all of it into one place and builds a single consolidated financial picture — a period-over-period P&L, EBITDA, the real cost of the workforce, and cash — then cross-checks the whole thing to surface what is missing or does not reconcile. This is the dashboard: one screen, every source, with the gaps flagged.

Take one worked example — the workforce. A single payroll event leaves three documents, and each tells only part of the story. The bank confirmation shows the net amount that was transferred. The payroll register holds the full employer cost. And the payslips break it down, person by person. Archon's Event Linker agent fuses all three into one accurate financial event. In the sample report, the bank moves about ten thousand seven hundred euros, while the true employer cost is over eighteen thousand — a gap of about seventy-two percent over the transfer. About half of that is the employer's own social-security contribution — roughly thirty-five percent over the transfer; the rest is the tax and social security withheld from the employee.

Here is where that money hides. Start from the bank transfer — the number most owners book as payroll. Add back the income tax and social-security contributions withheld from employees, and you reach gross pay. Add the employer's own social-security contributions on top, and you reach the true cost of employment. Across the corpus, the employer side alone adds about thirty-five percent over the bank figure; the employee withholdings add another thirty-six. Together they understate the workforce cost by about seventy-two percent — one hundred and thirty-three thousand euros, invisible until the three documents are correlated. And the workforce is just one of the aspects Archon reconciles this way.

Archon runs entirely on Nebius Serverless AI. Two on-demand CPU jobs do the heavy lifting — one extracts every document, one runs the financial analysis — and both shut themselves down the moment they finish. A CPU AI endpoint hosts the FastAPI backend that orchestrates them and submits the jobs through the Nebius Python SDK. The frontier models live in the Nebius Inference API: Qwen two-point-five Vision, with seventy-two billion parameters, reads scanned and multilingual documents; and Llama three-point-three, seventy billion, writes the executive summary. Object storage and managed PostgreSQL hold the raw files and the structured results. There is no always-on GPU — the containers stay cheap CPU instances, and the heavy compute lives in the inference layer.

We don't just claim accuracy — we measure it. Archon ships an evaluation harness, and it scores the real pipeline agents — the same Classifier, Event Linker, Validator, and P&L agents the product deploys, imported directly, not re-implemented — against a labelled corpus of forty synthetic cases. Every extracted number, date, classification, and fused total is checked against ground truth. One extractor reads the fields perfectly, to set the ceiling; a second, deliberately degraded extractor adds noise, to prove the metrics actually move. And the figure truth is scored separately from the validation truth, so a validation bug can never hide behind a correct number.

The results are concrete. Under perfect extraction, classification, field, and fusion accuracy all reach one hundred percent — the employer cost is reported to the cent, every time, across forty diverse cases. Validation outcomes reach one hundred percent too — but only after the harness caught a real defect, which we will come to. Now degrade the extractor: field accuracy falls into the seventies, but fusion accuracy collapses much faster, into the fifties, because small per-field errors compound through the fusion sums. That is exactly the signal a real extractor should be optimised against — and it is why the classifier earns its place.

This is the completeness engine — the part that tells you not just what the numbers are, but whether the picture is whole. Archon runs cross-document checks that reconcile independent sources against each other. Rule one reconciles the bank net against the sum of the payslip nets — the very same class of check that, extended across the ledger, catches a bank payment with no matching invoice. Rule three confirms the payment date falls inside the pay period. And here is where the harness earned its place: rules two and four used to never fire — not on a single case — because the register fields they read were never extracted, so a headcount mismatch slipped through silently. The harness measured that as zero firings out of thirty-seven. We wired those fields into the extractor, and the same harness now measures thirty-seven out of thirty-seven. The fix is proven, before and after — a number, not a hunch.

And every one of these numbers is reproducible from the README alone. The harness runs completely offline — no API key, no cloud, only pydantic — and scores the full forty-case corpus in about six seconds on a laptop, for zero euros, because the extractors are deterministic. Seed seven makes it byte-for-byte repeatable. The whole project sits above ninety percent test coverage, gated in continuous integration at a hard eighty-five percent floor — the harness, and the agents it scores, are themselves under test.

Archon turns a pile of raw documents into one consolidated financial picture — a boardroom-ready profit-and-loss statement — and tells you, with a measured answer, what is missing and whether your books actually reconcile.
